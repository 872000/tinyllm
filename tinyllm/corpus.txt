The lighthouse keeper woke before the sun. She climbed the iron stairs with a kettle in one hand and a book in the other. The sea was grey and calm, and the gulls were already arguing over the roof. She lit the lamp, checked the log, and wrote the date in a careful hand. The light turned slowly, and the boats far below turned with it.

In the village, the baker opened his shop at dawn. He mixed flour and water and salt, and he kneaded the dough until it was smooth. The smell of fresh bread drifted down the street, and the children ran to the door with coins in their pockets. The baker smiled and gave the smallest child an extra roll, warm from the oven.

A small robot lived in the workshop behind the bakery. It had learned to sweep the floor, to oil the hinges, and to sort the nails by size. One morning it found a recipe book under a pile of rags. It read the words slowly, one by one, and decided to bake a cake. The cake was lopsided and burnt at the edges, but the baker tasted it and laughed, and said it was a fine first cake.

The old train crossed the valley every evening at six. It carried letters, parcels, and passengers who waved from the windows. The station master knew every face and every name. He kept a notebook of arrivals and departures, and he never once lost a letter. When the train was late, he waited on the platform with his lantern, patient as the moon.

Mara kept a garden behind her house. She grew tomatoes, beans, and a row of sunflowers that stood taller than the fence. In summer she picked the tomatoes and made soup for her neighbours. In autumn she saved the seeds in paper envelopes and wrote the names on each one. In winter she planned the garden again, dreaming of spring.

The river ran cold and clear through the town. In the morning the fishermen cast their lines from the old bridge. In the afternoon the children skipped stones and counted the ripples. At night the river was quiet, and the stars were reflected in the water like a second sky.

An inventor built a clock that could tell the weather. It had hands of brass and a face of painted glass. When rain was coming, a small blue bird appeared in the window. When the sun was near, a golden bird took its place. The people of the town would look at the clock each morning before they chose their coats.

The library was the warmest room in the town. Its shelves were full of stories about sailors, farmers, singers, and stars. The librarian knew where every book belonged, and she could find a story for any mood. On rainy days the chairs were full, and the only sound was the turning of pages.

A young sailor learned to read the clouds. White clouds meant fair wind, grey clouds meant rain, and a red sky at night meant a fine day to follow. He taught his crew to watch the horizon, and they sailed farther than any boat from the town had sailed before. They returned with maps, shells, and stories of islands no one had named.

The market square woke with the sound of carts. Farmers brought apples, cheese, and honey. A potter set out bowls glazed the colour of the sea. A weaver hung scarves that fluttered like flags. By noon the square was full of voices, and by dusk it was empty again, swept clean for the next day.
